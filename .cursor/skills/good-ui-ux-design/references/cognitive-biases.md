# 106 Cognitive Biases & Principles That Affect UX

Source: Growth.Design Psychology Reference (https://growth.design/psychology)

Every time users interact with your product, they: (1) Filter information, (2) Seek meaning, (3) Act within a given time, (4) Store bits in memory. Below are the biases and principles organized by these four categories.

## 1. Information (Filtering)

Users filter out a lot of the information that they receive, even when it could be important.

| # | Principle | Summary | UX Application |
|---|-----------|---------|----------------|
| 1 | **Hick's Law** | More options leads to harder decisions | Reduce choices. Simplify menus. Use progressive disclosure. |
| 2 | **Confirmation Bias** | People look for evidence that confirms what they think | Personalize content to match user expectations; be careful not to create filter bubbles. |
| 3 | **Priming** | Previous stimuli influence users' decisions | Use imagery, copy, and color before key decisions to set the right mental frame. |
| 4 | **Cognitive Load** | Total mental effort required to complete a task | Minimize fields, steps, and visual noise. Break complex tasks into chunks. |
| 5 | **Anchoring Bias** | Users rely heavily on the first piece of information they see | Show the most important/favorable number first (e.g., original price before discount). |
| 6 | **Nudge** | Subtle hints can affect users' decisions | Use default selections, visual cues, and gentle prompts to guide behavior without forcing. |
| 7 | **Progressive Disclosure** | Users are less overwhelmed if exposed to complex features later | Show basic features first; reveal advanced options as users gain comfort. |
| 8 | **Fitts's Law** | Large and close elements are easier to interact with | Make primary CTAs large and easily reachable. Place important actions in thumb zones on mobile. |
| 9 | **Banner Blindness** | Users tune out stuff they get repeatedly exposed to | Avoid placing important info in typical ad positions. Vary presentation of recurring elements. |
| 10 | **Decoy Effect** | Create a new option that's easy to discard | Add a strategically inferior option to make the target option look more attractive. |
| 11 | **Framing** | How information is presented affects decisions | "95% fat-free" vs "5% fat" — same info, different perception. Frame positively. |
| 12 | **Attentional Bias** | Users' thoughts filter what they pay attention to | Align messaging with what's already on users' minds (context, season, recent events). |
| 13 | **Empathy Gap** | People underestimate how emotions influence behaviors | Design for emotional states, not just rational ones. Test with stressed/tired users. |
| 14 | **Visual Anchors** | Elements used to guide users' eyes | Use arrows, gaze direction in images, whitespace, and contrast to direct attention. |
| 15 | **Von Restorff Effect** | People notice items that stand out more | Make your key CTA visually distinct from surrounding elements. |
| 16 | **Visual Hierarchy** | The order in which people perceive what they see | Use size, color, contrast, and spacing to establish clear scanning order. |
| 17 | **Selective Attention** | People filter out things from their environment when in focus | Don't rely on peripheral elements for critical information when users are task-focused. |
| 18 | **Survivorship Bias** | People neglect things that don't make it past selection | Don't only study successful users — understand why dropoffs happen. |
| 19 | **Juxtaposition** | Close, similar elements are perceived as a single unit | Group related items visually. Separate unrelated items with whitespace. |
| 20 | **Signifiers** | Elements that communicate what they will do | Buttons should look clickable. Links should look like links. Affordances matter. |
| 21 | **Contrast** | Attention is drawn to higher visual weights | Use contrast strategically to highlight primary actions and de-emphasize secondary ones. |
| 22 | **External Trigger** | When info on what to do next is within the prompt itself | Make CTAs self-explanatory. "Start free trial" > "Submit". |
| 23 | **Centre-Stage Effect** | People tend to choose the middle option in a set | Place your preferred/recommended plan in the center of a pricing table. |
| 24 | **Law of Proximity** | Close elements are usually considered related | Use spacing to create logical groupings. Don't separate related form fields. |
| 25 | **Tesler's Law** | Simplify too much and you transfer complexity to users | There's an irreducible minimum complexity — make sure you absorb it, not the user. |
| 26 | **Spark Effect** | Users are more likely to act when the effort is small | Reduce friction for initial actions. Pre-fill fields. Enable one-click actions. |
| 27 | **Feedback Loop** | When users act, feedback communicates what happened | Always show immediate response to user actions — loading states, confirmations, progress. |

## 2. Meaning (Interpreting)

When users try to give sense to information, they make stories and assumptions to fill the gaps.

| # | Principle | Summary | UX Application |
|---|-----------|---------|----------------|
| 28 | **Social Proof** | Users adapt behaviors based on what others do | Show reviews, user counts, testimonials, "popular" badges. |
| 29 | **Scarcity** | People value things more when in limited supply | "Only 3 left" or time-limited offers — but use REAL scarcity, not fake urgency. |
| 30 | **Curiosity Gap** | Users desire to seek out missing information | Tease content with partial information to drive engagement ("See what happened next..."). |
| 31 | **Mental Model** | Users have preconceived opinions of how things work | Match existing conventions. Don't reinvent navigation or common patterns without good reason. |
| 32 | **Familiarity Bias** | People prefer familiar experiences | Use established UI patterns. Innovate on value, not on basic interaction paradigms. |
| 33 | **Skeuomorphism** | Users adapt more easily to things resembling real-world objects | Use real-world metaphors for novel features (trash can for delete, folder for organization). |
| 34 | **Reciprocity** | People feel the need to reciprocate when they receive something | Give value first (free tools, content, trials) before asking for commitment. |
| 35 | **Singularity Effect** | Users care more about an individual than a group | Tell one person's story rather than showing aggregate statistics to drive emotional engagement. |
| 36 | **Variable Reward** | People especially enjoy unexpected rewards | Add surprise elements — random rewards, Easter eggs, unexpected celebrations. |
| 37 | **Aha! Moment** | When new users first realize your product's value | Identify your product's core value moment and engineer the shortest path to it during onboarding. |
| 38 | **Goal Gradient Effect** | Motivation increases as users get closer to their goal | Show progress bars. Start them slightly filled (e.g., "Profile 20% complete"). |
| 39 | **Occam's Razor** | Simple solutions are often better than complex ones | When in doubt, simplify. Remove features before adding them. |
| 40 | **Noble Edge Effect** | Users prefer socially responsible companies | Communicate your social mission authentically — but only if genuine. |
| 41 | **Hawthorne Effect** | Users change behavior when they know they're observed | Be transparent about analytics. Use observation effects positively (e.g., fitness tracking). |
| 42 | **Halo Effect** | People judge things based on feelings toward one trait | First impressions matter enormously. Polish the first touchpoint users see. |
| 43 | **Miller's Law** | Users can keep 7 plus or minus 2 items in working memory | Limit options, menu items, and displayed information to ~5-9 items. |
| 44 | **Unit Bias** | One unit of something feels like the optimal amount | Design "natural" content units (one article, one lesson, one episode). |
| 45 | **Flow State** | Being fully immersed and focused on a task | Remove interruptions during key tasks. Match challenge to skill level. |
| 46 | **Authority Bias** | Users attribute more importance to authority figures | Use expert endorsements, certifications, and authoritative design language. |
| 47 | **Pseudo-Set Framing** | Tasks part of a group are more tempting to complete | Bundle features into collections/series to increase completion motivation. |
| 48 | **Group Attractiveness Effect** | Individual items seem more attractive in a group | Present features/products as curated collections rather than isolated items. |
| 49 | **Curse of Knowledge** | Not realizing people don't share your level of knowledge | Write for beginners. Test with new users. Avoid jargon. |
| 50 | **Self-Initiated Triggers** | Users interact more with prompts they set up themselves | Let users create their own reminders and notifications rather than forcing defaults. |
| 51 | **Survey Bias** | Users skew survey answers toward what's socially acceptable | Use anonymous surveys. Ask behavioral questions, not opinion questions. |
| 52 | **Cognitive Dissonance** | Painful to hold two opposing ideas in our mind | Don't contradict the user's self-image. Align messaging with their identity. |
| 53 | **Feedforward** | When users know what to expect before acting | Preview outcomes before users commit (e.g., "This will send to 250 contacts"). |
| 54 | **Hindsight Bias** | People overestimate their ability to predict outcomes | Don't blame users for mistakes. Design forgiving interfaces with undo. |
| 55 | **Law of Similarity** | Users perceive a relationship between similar-looking elements | Make functionally similar elements look similar. Make different functions look different. |
| 56 | **Law of Pragnanz** | Users interpret ambiguous images in simpler form | Use clean, simple shapes and layouts. Ambiguity creates cognitive strain. |
| 57 | **Streisand Effect** | Censoring information increases awareness of it | Don't draw attention to what you want to hide. Address issues transparently. |
| 58 | **Spotlight Effect** | People believe they're noticed more than they are | Users worry about social judgment — reduce visibility of mistakes/failures in social features. |
| 59 | **Fresh Start Effect** | Users are more likely to act with a feeling of new beginnings | Time campaigns around new years, Mondays, birthdays, account anniversaries. |
| 60 | **Expectations Bias** | People tend to be influenced by their own expectations | Set appropriate expectations before experiences to influence perception positively. |
| 61 | **Aesthetic-Usability Effect** | Beautiful designs are perceived as easier to use | Invest in visual polish — it literally makes your product feel more functional. |

## 3. Time (Acting)

Users are busy so they look for shortcuts and jump to conclusions quickly.

| # | Principle | Summary | UX Application |
|---|-----------|---------|----------------|
| 62 | **Labor Illusion** | People value things more when they see the work behind them | Show processing animations, search effort, or "finding the best results for you..." |
| 63 | **Default Bias** | Users tend not to change established behavior | Set smart defaults that benefit users (opt-in to good things, opt-out of bad). |
| 64 | **Investment Loops** | Users who invest are more likely to come back | Encourage users to customize, save, create — stored value increases retention. |
| 65 | **Loss Aversion** | People prefer avoiding losses over gaining equivalent | Frame as "Don't lose your progress" rather than "Keep going to gain a reward." |
| 66 | **Commitment & Consistency** | Users tend to be consistent with previous actions | Start with small asks, then escalate. Remind users of their past choices to encourage follow-through. |
| 67 | **Sunk Cost Effect** | Users are reluctant to abandon something they're invested in | Show users how much they've already invested (time, data, connections) before they churn. |
| 68 | **Reactance** | Users are less likely to adopt behavior when forced | Give users choice and autonomy. Avoid aggressive pop-ups and forced actions. |
| 69 | **Law of the Instrument** | If all you have is a hammer, everything looks like a nail | Don't over-rely on one solution pattern. Match the tool to the actual problem. |
| 70 | **Temptation Bundling** | Hard tasks are less scary when coupled with desired things | Pair difficult/boring tasks with enjoyable elements (e.g., learn while earning badges). |
| 71 | **Dunning-Kruger Effect** | People overestimate skills when they don't know much | Beginners need guardrails they don't know they need. Guide without patronizing. |
| 72 | **Discoverability** | The ease with which users can discover features | Don't hide critical features. Use contextual hints, tooltips, and onboarding tours. |
| 73 | **Second-Order Effect** | The consequences of the consequences of actions | Think beyond immediate impact. What happens when millions of users do this? |
| 74 | **Decision Fatigue** | Many decisions lowers ability to make rational ones | Front-load important decisions. Reduce trivial choices later in flows. |
| 75 | **Observer-Expectancy Effect** | Researchers' biases influence experiment participants | In user research, use neutral language and blind testing where possible. |
| 76 | **Weber's Law** | Users adapt better to small incremental changes | Roll out changes gradually. Avoid jarring redesigns. |
| 77 | **Parkinson's Law** | Tasks take as much time as allowed | Set reasonable deadlines and timeframes in UI (e.g., "takes 2 min" for forms). |
| 78 | **Affect Heuristic** | Current emotions cloud and influence judgment | Design for emotional states. A frustrated user processes information differently. |
| 79 | **Hyperbolic Discounting** | People prioritize immediate benefits over bigger future gains | Offer immediate value/rewards alongside long-term benefits. |
| 80 | **Chronoception** | Perception of time is subjective | Use animations and progress indicators to make waits feel shorter. |
| 81 | **Cashless Effect** | People spend more when they can't see the money | Be aware of this in pricing UX — make costs transparent and clear to be ethical. |
| 82 | **Self-Serving Bias** | People take credit for wins, blame others for losses | Celebrate user successes ("You did it!"). Take blame for errors ("We're sorry, something went wrong"). |
| 83 | **Pareto Principle** | 80% of effects come from 20% of causes | Focus design effort on the 20% of features that deliver 80% of value. |
| 84 | **Backfire Effect** | Challenged convictions get stronger | Don't aggressively counter user beliefs. Gently provide alternative perspectives. |
| 85 | **False Consensus Effect** | People overestimate how much others agree with them | Don't assume your team's preferences match users'. Always validate with research. |
| 86 | **Bandwagon Effect** | Users adopt beliefs in proportion to others who have | Show adoption metrics ("Join 10M+ users") to leverage social momentum. |
| 87 | **Barnum-Forer Effect** | Believing generic descriptions apply specifically to you | Personalization that feels specific ("Based on your preferences...") drives engagement. |
| 88 | **IKEA Effect** | Partially creating something makes users value it more | Let users customize, configure, and co-create within your product. |
| 89 | **Planning Fallacy** | People underestimate how long tasks will take | Show realistic time estimates. Under-promise and over-deliver on completion times. |

## 4. Memory (Storing)

Users try to remember what's most important, but their brain prefers some elements over others.

| # | Principle | Summary | UX Application |
|---|-----------|---------|----------------|
| 90 | **Provide Exit Points** | Invite users to leave your app at the right moment | Offer natural stopping points. This builds trust and reduces guilt/addiction. |
| 91 | **Peak-End Rule** | People judge experiences by the peak and how they end | Engineer the highest moment and a strong positive ending. |
| 92 | **Sensory Appeal** | Users engage more with multi-sensory experiences | Use haptic feedback, sound, animation, and rich media to create memorable moments. |
| 93 | **Zeigarnik Effect** | People remember incomplete tasks better than completed ones | Use progress indicators and "resume where you left off" to bring users back. |
| 94 | **Endowment Effect** | Users value something more if they feel it's theirs | Let users try/customize before buying. "Your custom plan" > "Plan B." |
| 95 | **Chunking** | People remember grouped information better | Break content into digestible groups (phone numbers, credit card fields, step numbers). |
| 96 | **Delighters** | People remember unexpected, playful pleasures more | Add micro-interactions, celebrations, humor — small moments that spark joy. |
| 97 | **Internal Trigger** | Users prompted to act based on a memory/emotion | Build associations between user emotions and your product (bored -> opens app). |
| 98 | **Picture Superiority Effect** | People remember pictures better than words | Use visuals and icons to reinforce key messages, especially in onboarding. |
| 99 | **Method of Loci** | Things associated with a location are remembered better | Use spatial layout consistently so users remember where features live. |
| 100 | **Shaping** | Incrementally reinforcing actions toward target behavior | Reward small steps toward desired behavior, not just the final outcome. |
| 101 | **Recognition Over Recall** | Easier to recognize things than recall from memory | Show options rather than requiring users to type from memory (dropdowns > free text). |
| 102 | **Storytelling Effect** | People remember stories better than facts alone | Wrap data and features in narratives. Case studies > feature lists. |
| 103 | **Negativity Bias** | Users recall negative events more than positive ones | One bad experience outweighs many good ones. Prioritize fixing pain points. |
| 104 | **Availability Heuristic** | Users favor recent/available info over past info | Recent touchpoints shape perception most — make the latest interaction great. |
| 105 | **Spacing Effect** | People learn more when study sessions are spaced out | Space out onboarding education rather than front-loading everything at once. |
| 106 | **Serial Position Effect** | Easier to recall first and last items of a list | Put the most important items first and last in any list or navigation. |

## How to Use This Reference

**For UX Audits**: Walk through each category and ask which biases are being triggered (positively or negatively) at each step of the user flow.

**For Feature Design**: Identify which of the 4 stages (Information, Meaning, Time, Memory) your feature primarily impacts, then review the relevant biases.

**For Ethical Review**: Check if any biases are being exploited against the user's interest. Cross-reference with the ethical tests in the main SKILL.md.

**For Team Communication**: Use the principle names as a shared vocabulary ("We're leveraging the Goal Gradient Effect here" or "This creates unnecessary Cognitive Load").
