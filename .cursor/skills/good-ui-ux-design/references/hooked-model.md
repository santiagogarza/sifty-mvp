# The Hooked Model — Building Habit-Forming Products

Source: Nir Eyal's "Hooked: How to Build Habit-Forming Products"

The Hooked Model is a four-step loop that moves users from needing external reminders (ads, notifications) to using a product automatically because of internal feelings (boredom, loneliness, curiosity, anxiety). The goal is to create products so tied to users' emotions and routines that using them becomes second nature.

A habit is an automatic behavior triggered by situational cues — no conscious decision required. Products that successfully create habits enjoy higher lifetime value, greater pricing flexibility, supercharged growth, and a sharper competitive edge.

## The Four Phases

### Phase 1: Trigger — "The Spark Plug"

The trigger is what initiates the behavior. There are two types:

**External Triggers** — Information in the user's environment telling them what to do next:
- **Paid triggers**: Ads, SEM, paid social. Effective for acquisition but not sustainable for habits.
- **Earned triggers**: Press mentions, viral videos, app store features. Attention-grabbing but hard to maintain.
- **Relationship triggers**: Word of mouth, shares, invites. One person engaging another.
- **Owned triggers**: Push notifications, email newsletters, app icon on home screen. The most important — these show up daily without ongoing cost.

**Internal Triggers** — Emotions, routines, or situations that become associated with the product:
- Negative emotions are the most powerful: boredom, loneliness, frustration, confusion, anxiety, FOMO
- The product becomes the user's automatic response to that feeling
- Example: Feeling bored → open TikTok. Feeling uncertain → Google it. Feeling lonely → open Instagram.

**Design goal**: Start with external triggers, but engineer the experience so that internal triggers form over time. The product should become the "default response" to a recurring emotional state.

**How to identify your internal trigger**: Ask "Why?" five times. Why does the user open the app? Because they're bored. Why are they bored? Because they're waiting in line. Why does waiting bother them? Because they feel unproductive. The root emotion — feeling unproductive — is your internal trigger.

### Phase 2: Action — "The Simplest Behavior in Anticipation of a Reward"

The action is the simplest behavior the user performs expecting a reward. It follows the Fogg Behavior Model:

**B = M × A × P** (Behavior = Motivation × Ability × Prompt)

For the action to occur:
- **Motivation must exist** (the trigger created desire)
- **Ability must be high** (the action must be dead simple)
- **A prompt must be present** (the trigger tells them what to do)

**Key principle: Always increase Ability before increasing Motivation.** Making something easier is more reliable than making someone want it more. Technology's job is to reduce friction.

**The 6 Elements of Simplicity** (what makes an action "easy"):
1. **Time** — How long does it take? (Shorter = easier)
2. **Money** — What's the financial cost? (Cheaper = easier)
3. **Physical effort** — How much exertion? (Less = easier)
4. **Brain cycles** — How much thinking? (Less = easier)
5. **Social deviance** — How socially acceptable? (More accepted = easier)
6. **Non-routine** — How different from habits? (More familiar = easier)

**The action is the scarcest resource bottleneck.** Find which of these six is the biggest barrier for your user and eliminate it.

**Examples of ultra-simple actions**:
- Tinder: Swipe right (one thumb gesture, zero cognitive load)
- TikTok: Just keep scrolling (infinite, effortless)
- Google: Type a few words (from any device, instant)
- Pinterest: Scroll and tap to save (visual, no reading required)
- Twitter/X: Pull down to refresh (muscle memory)

### Phase 3: Variable Reward — "The Unpredictable Payoff"

This is the engine of the hook. The user gets what they came for, but with a twist of unpredictability. Research on the nucleus accumbens shows the brain lights up more in *anticipation* of a reward than in receiving it. Unpredictability sustains that anticipation.

If rewards are always the same, they become boring (hedonic adaptation). Variability keeps the brain engaged.

#### Three Types of Variable Rewards

**1. Rewards of the Tribe (Social)**
The need for social connection, belonging, acceptance, and validation.
- Instagram: Likes, comments, story views — you never know how many you'll get
- Stack Overflow: Upvotes and reputation points from peers
- Facebook: Who liked your post? Who commented? What did they say?
- Slack/Discord: Who's talking? What's the energy in the channel?
- Dating apps: Who matched with you? The anticipation of opening the app.

**2. Rewards of the Hunt (Resources/Information)**
The primal drive to acquire resources, information, or deals — the thrill of the chase.
- TikTok/Instagram: Scrolling a feed — what will the next post be? (Information foraging)
- Twitter/X: What's happening right now? Breaking news? Hot takes?
- Amazon: Deal hunting, lightning sales, price drops
- Slot machines/Loot boxes: The classic variable reward mechanic
- Google: Each search might surface the exact answer or send you down a rabbit hole
- Shopping apps: Temu, Wish — the next scroll might reveal something amazing and cheap

**3. Rewards of the Self (Mastery/Completion)**
The personal satisfaction of competence, consistency, and completion.
- Duolingo: Streak maintenance, leveling up, XP earned
- Video games: Points, achievements, clearing levels
- Email: Inbox zero — the satisfaction of clearing all messages
- Strava: Personal records, new distance milestones
- Coding: Getting a test to pass, shipping a feature, solving a bug
- Fitness apps: Completing a workout, closing Apple Watch rings

**The best products layer multiple reward types.** Instagram hits all three: Tribe (likes/comments), Hunt (what's on the feed?), Self (growing follower count, aesthetic grid).

### Phase 4: Investment — "Loading the Next Trigger"

The user puts something *back* into the product — time, data, effort, social capital, or money. This phase does two critical things:

1. **Increases stored value** — Unlike physical products that depreciate, habit-forming products appreciate with use. The more you invest, the better it gets, and the harder it is to leave.
2. **Loads the next trigger** — The investment creates the conditions for the next external trigger, restarting the loop.

**Types of Investment**:
- **Content**: Uploading photos, writing posts, creating playlists (Instagram, Spotify, Pinterest)
- **Data**: Profile information, preferences, history (Netflix, Amazon, Spotify)
- **Followers/Connections**: Building a social graph (Twitter, LinkedIn, TikTok)
- **Reputation**: Reviews, karma, followers, stars (Yelp, Reddit, GitHub, Uber driver rating)
- **Skill**: Learning the interface, keyboard shortcuts, workflows (Figma, Notion, Vim)
- **Money**: Subscription, in-app purchases, credits (any SaaS)

**Key Investment Patterns**:
- Following an account → you'll get a notification when they post (next trigger)
- Posting a story → you'll check back for views and replies (next trigger)
- Saving a song → your Discover Weekly gets better (improved reward)
- Rating movies → Netflix recommendations improve (improved reward)
- Adding tasks → you'll return to check them off (Zeigarnik Effect + next trigger)

**Investment should happen AFTER the reward, not before.** Users are most willing to invest when they've just received value. Don't ask for investment during onboarding — ask after the first Aha Moment.

## The Habit Zone

A product enters the "Habit Zone" when it achieves sufficient **frequency** and **perceived utility**:

- **High frequency + High perceived utility** = Strong habit (checking email, social media)
- **High frequency + Low perceived utility** = Compulsion/addiction risk (mindless scrolling)
- **Low frequency + High perceived utility** = Tool, not a habit (booking flights, filing taxes)
- **Low frequency + Low perceived utility** = Forgettable (most apps that get uninstalled)

The sweet spot for habit formation: **the behavior happens at least weekly** (ideally daily) and the user **perceives genuine value** each time.

## Real-World Breakdowns

### Instagram
| Phase | What Happens |
|-------|-------------|
| **Trigger** | Internal: boredom, loneliness, FOMO. External: push notification ("X just posted a story") |
| **Action** | Open app, scroll feed (zero friction — muscle memory) |
| **Variable Reward** | Tribe: likes/comments on your post. Hunt: funny meme, friend's vacation photos. Self: follower growth |
| **Investment** | Post a photo, follow someone, DM a friend → loads next trigger (replies, likes, story views) |

### Duolingo
| Phase | What Happens |
|-------|-------------|
| **Trigger** | Internal: guilt about not learning. External: streak reminder notification |
| **Action** | Open app, tap "Start Lesson" (one tap to begin, lessons are 2-3 min) |
| **Variable Reward** | Self: XP, streak count, league rank. Tribe: leaderboard position vs friends. Hunt: new content |
| **Investment** | Complete a lesson → streak grows, harder to abandon. Follow friends → social accountability |

### Tinder
| Phase | What Happens |
|-------|-------------|
| **Trigger** | Internal: loneliness, boredom, curiosity. External: "You have a new match!" |
| **Action** | Swipe (the lowest-friction decision mechanism ever designed — one thumb gesture) |
| **Variable Reward** | Tribe: a match! Someone likes you. Hunt: who's next? Self: validation, attractiveness confirmation |
| **Investment** | Set up profile, add photos, write bio → better matches. Super Like someone → loads trigger for response |

### Slack
| Phase | What Happens |
|-------|-------------|
| **Trigger** | Internal: "Did someone message me?" External: desktop/mobile notification |
| **Action** | Click notification, read message (instant, familiar) |
| **Variable Reward** | Tribe: team banter, recognition. Hunt: important info, decisions. Self: being responsive, helpful |
| **Investment** | Create channels, pin messages, build integrations → Slack becomes indispensable infrastructure |

## How to Apply the Hooked Model

### When Designing a New Product
1. **Identify the internal trigger**: What recurring negative emotion does your product address? Use the "5 Whys" technique to find the root emotion.
2. **Define the simplest action**: What is the absolute minimum the user must do to get a reward? Reduce it further. Then reduce it again.
3. **Design variable rewards**: Layer at least 2 of the 3 reward types (Tribe, Hunt, Self). Make sure rewards are genuine and valuable, not manufactured.
4. **Plan the investment**: What does the user put back in? How does it improve their next experience? How does it load the next trigger?

### When Auditing an Existing Product
Ask for each phase:
- **Trigger**: What internal trigger are we tied to? Is there a clear emotion → product association? Are our external triggers well-timed and valuable (not spammy)?
- **Action**: How many taps/clicks to the first reward? What's the biggest friction point? Which of the 6 simplicity elements is the bottleneck?
- **Variable Reward**: Is the reward genuinely variable or predictable? Which reward types are present? Are users getting bored (hedonic adaptation)?
- **Investment**: Are users storing value? What makes them come back? How does their investment improve the next loop?

### Red Flags (Your Hook Is Broken)
- Users need constant external triggers (ads, emails) to return → no internal trigger formed
- High drop-off on the action step → too much friction
- Users try once but don't return → reward wasn't variable or valuable enough
- Low engagement after initial onboarding → no investment loop, no stored value

## Ethical Considerations

Nir Eyal himself emphasizes the "Manipulation Matrix" — a framework for evaluating whether building habits is ethical:

| | Maker Uses It | Maker Doesn't Use It |
|---|---|---|
| **Materially Improves User's Life** | **Facilitator** (ethical) | **Peddler** (risky) |
| **Doesn't Improve User's Life** | **Entertainer** (gray area) | **Dealer** (unethical) |

**The Facilitator** is the ideal: you use your own product AND it materially improves users' lives (e.g., Duolingo, Strava, Notion).

**The Dealer** is the worst: you wouldn't use it yourself AND it doesn't help users (e.g., predatory gambling apps, engagement-bait social features).

**Rules for ethical habit design**:
- The product should genuinely improve the user's life with repeated use
- Variable rewards should be real value, not manufactured dopamine hits
- Investment should make the product better for the user, not just stickier for the business
- Always provide exit points and usage awareness tools
- Ask: "Would I be comfortable if my family used this product exactly as designed?"
